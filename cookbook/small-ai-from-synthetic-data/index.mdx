# Practical Guide to Small AI from Synthetic Data

**A cookbook for data science teams in national statistical organizations and other data producers.** Version 0.1 (working draft), October 2026.

:::note[Working draft]
This guide is a working draft from the AI for Data – Data for AI program. It
collects practical approaches and open standards. It does not
represent official World Bank Group guidance or policy and is not legal
advice; an organization applies it under its own statistical law, data
protection rules, and institutional policies.
Comments and corrections are welcome as
[GitHub issues](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/issues), and the
[contributing](./contributing.md) page explains how to propose a recipe.
:::

Small AI, in this guide, means a model with tens or hundreds of millions
of parameters, trained for one narrow task and run on the organization's
own hardware. Such a model can assign topics to documents, code a
response, or find a type of entity in text, at a fraction of a second
per document and without sending the text outside the organization. Its
training needs labelled examples, and few organizations have thousands
of them for a new task. This guide uses a large language model to write
those examples. The large model is used during development, and the
small model then runs alone.

The guide follows one task from start to finish: assigning topics from
the World Bank's topic list to working papers. Its seven chapters
answer the questions a team asks along the way: which task suits the
approach and what its labels are, how the examples are generated and
checked, how the small model is trained, how well it does on real
documents, what running it over a collection costs, and how the model
and its data are documented and kept current. Every figure in the
chapters comes from running the guide's scripts on public data, and the
results include the ones that did not work as expected.

The figure below steps through the seven chapters with the figures of
the running example. The dashed arrow marks the one step that returns
to an earlier one: a label that the model suggests too often is traced
to the training data, and the examples are generated again with the cause removed.

The recipes build on open tools: the OpenAI chat completions interface,
which hosted services and local model servers offer, for generation;
Hugging Face Transformers for training; and scikit-learn for
evaluation. The [standards page](./standards.md) lists each and where it
applies. The [Small and Open Models cookbook](/cookbook/small-and-open-models/)
covers the choice, serving, and security of small models in general;
this guide covers one way of making one.

## Prerequisites

The recipes assume the following are in place or can be arranged:

- A narrow task with a fixed list of labels, and a collection of documents the task applies to.
- A data scientist with Python, access to a GPU for training (a free hosted notebook is enough for the model sizes in this guide), and access to a large language model through a hosted service or a local server.
- A task owner who knows the subject and can review labels and examples, and a legal or procurement officer who can read a model licence.
- For the AI-ready and AI-native levels, a source of real documents with labels assigned independently of the model, and a place to publish models and data with their cards.

## First steps

For a team with limited time, this sequence gives the most useful result first:

1. Write the task statement and the label file ([chapter 1](./task.mdx)). This takes half a day.
2. Build a real test set of a few hundred documents and score a large model on it ([chapter 5](./evaluate.mdx), recipes 5.1 and 5.3). This takes one to three days, and it says what quality to expect before any training.
3. Generate and check a first set of examples ([chapters 2](./generate.mdx) and [3](./check.mdx)). This takes a day and costs a few dollars.
4. Train the small model and compare it with the large model on the same documents ([chapter 4](./train.mdx) and recipe 5.3). This takes a day.

The application to the collection ([chapter 6](./apply.mdx)) and the
cards and retraining rule ([chapter 7](./release.mdx)) follow once the
task owner has accepted the comparison.

## Maturity levels

Each chapter describes three levels. An organization can sit at different
levels in different chapters, and the first level is a complete, useful
state on its own.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | The practice exists in a form a person can use and a crawler can read. |
| **AI-ready** | The practice is structured, identified, and accessible through documented interfaces, so that software, including AI systems, can use it without manual steps. |
| **AI-native** | The practice offers AI-oriented interfaces or methods, with provenance and verification built in. |

## Running example

The running example of this guide uses public material in place of
the fictional organization of the other cookbooks, because the method
can only be shown on real documents:

- The label list is the World Bank's topic list as published on its [topic page](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic), retrieved in 2024: 95 names that become 86 labels.
- The earlier generated set is the public dataset [avsolatorio/doc-topics-synthetic_data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/avsolatorio/doc-topics-synthetic_data): 41,416 sentences written in 2024 by GPT-4 and Llama 3 70B for the [doc-topics](https://github.com/avsolatorio/doc-topics) project.
- The new generated sets were written for this guide in October 2026 by gpt-oss-120b, an open-weight model under the Apache 2.0 licence.
- The real documents are the abstracts of the 1,922 Policy Research Working Papers published from 2016 to 2020, with their subjects, from the World Bank [Documents and Reports API](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/api).
- The small model is DeBERTa-v3-small, published by Microsoft under the MIT licence.

The generated sentences are invented and say so in their dataset card.
The abstracts and subjects are quoted from the World Bank's catalogue.

## Scope of the examples

The example covers one task, one language, and one pretrained encoder,
with one training run per configuration unless a recipe says otherwise.
Differences of about 0.03 in micro F1 between configurations are within
the variation between runs, and the chapters say so where it matters.
Each recipe ends with a **What this does not do** note that says where
the example stops and what the full version adds.
