# Practical Guide to Metadata Curation with Language Models

**A cookbook for metadata curators and their managers in national statistical organizations.** Version 0.1 (working draft), October 2026.

:::note[Working draft]
This guide is a working draft from the AI for Data – Data for AI program. It
collects practical approaches and open standards. Comments and corrections
are welcome as [GitHub issues](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/issues),
and the [contributing](./contributing.md) page explains how to propose a
recipe.
:::

Metadata curation is skilled work that never ends: thousands of records,
each with dozens of fields, written by different people over years, with
definitions that drift and keywords that follow no list. Language models
can draft a missing description from the record and the schema, score a
record on completeness and consistency, flag a name that does not match
its definition, and map free-text keywords to a vocabulary. They can also
produce plausible text that is wrong. The difference between help and harm
is the workflow: what the model is asked to do, how its output is checked,
who decides, and how decisions feed back.

This guide is organized around six questions a curation team can ask:
which tasks a model can assist and which stay with people, whether a model
can draft fields a curator will accept, whether it can assess quality
consistently, how curators review and decide on suggestions, whether free
text can be aligned with controlled vocabularies, and how curator
decisions improve the system. Each chapter answers one question with
recipes, maturity levels, and a checklist.

The recipes build on the program's
[Generative AI for Metadata Quality](/docs/metadata-quality/generative-ai-for-metadata-quality)
work, which scores records on four dimensions with structured output, and
on the [Metadata Reviewer](/docs/metadata-reviewer/overview), an agentic
pipeline that detects issues, categorizes them, scores their severity, and
proposes corrections for a curator's review board. Records follow the
[World Bank metadata schemas](https://github.com/worldbank/metadata-schemas).
The [standards page](./standards.md) lists each standard and where it
applies.

:::tip[The curator decides]
Nothing in this guide publishes model output. Every draft, score, and
mapping is a suggestion that a named curator accepts, edits, or rejects,
and the record of those decisions is what makes the system better over
time. A workflow that skips the decision is faster and worse.
:::

## Maturity levels

Each chapter describes three levels. A team can sit at different levels in
different chapters, and the first level is a complete, useful state on its
own.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Curation rules and decision rights are written; models are used for drafts and flags on a small set of records, with every output reviewed. |
| **AI-ready** | Drafting, assessment, and mapping run with structured output over the catalog, calibrated against curators, reviewed through a board, with decisions recorded. |
| **AI-native** | Decisions feed an evaluation set that gates changes to prompts and models; acceptance rates and error taxonomies are tracked per field and model; small or local models handle routine tasks. |

## Running example

The example organization of the dissemination cookbook has five indicator
records. One lacks a definition, one has a unit that does not match its
values, one has a name that does not match its definition, and two have
free-text keywords that follow no list. The recipes draft the missing
definition, score the five records on four dimensions and compare the
scores with a curator's, run the records through a review pipeline, map
the keywords to a small vocabulary, and compute acceptance rates from the
curator's decisions. All records and decisions are invented.

## Scope of the examples

The examples are small on purpose: five records, ten suggestions, fourteen
keywords, so that a reader can run them in an afternoon and see the shape
of each check. They are starting points. Each recipe ends with a **What
this does not do** note that says where the example stops and what the
full version adds. The model calls themselves use the program's tools; the
scripts here measure what comes back.
