# 6. Review and accessibility

**Question:** How is a video checked and made accessible before it is published?

## Rationale

A video is harder to check than a page. Its figures pass at the speed of
the voice, its text appears for a few seconds, and a fault in one beat can
hide behind the motion of the next. The checks of the earlier chapters
cover the parts: the fact sheet, the narration, and the stage. The review
covers the finished video, as a viewer meets it, and it ends with a
person who takes responsibility for its publication in the
organization's name.

Accessibility is part of the same review. The Web Content Accessibility
Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 require captions for the audio of prerecorded video
at level A (success criterion 1.2.2). They require audio description of
the video content, or a media alternative such as a full transcript, at
level A (1.2.3), and audio description at level AA (1.2.5). The W3C's
explanation of these criteria states that where all of the video
information is already provided in the existing audio, no additional
audio description is necessary. A narration that describes what is on
screen, as chapter 3 requires, therefore serves viewers who cannot see
the screen. A transcript published with the video meets the media
alternative of level AAA (1.2.8) and lets a reader search and quote the
video.

## Target state

- Every figure in the video, in the captions, and on screen has been compared with the source by a person and by script.
- The whole video has been watched and heard once with sound and once with captions and no sound.
- The captions pass the caption check, and the captions and a transcript are published with the video.
- A named person has signed off the video, and the sign-off is recorded with the checks behind the decision.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Run the four recipes of this chapter on every video before publication. |
| **AI-ready** | Keep the sign-off record of recipe 6.4 with the video's files and the catalogue record, and run the narration and caption checks on every version. |
| **AI-native** | Run the checks of chapters 3 and 6 automatically on every change to the step script or the captions, and block publication until they pass and the sign-off is recorded. |

## Recipes

<Recipe title="6.1 Compare every figure with the source, by person and by script" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination officer and the owner of the source" time="An hour per video">

**Result:** every figure that the video states, in the narration, in the
captions, and on screen, has been compared with the release table by the
narration check and by a person who did not write the narration.

The narration check of chapter 3 compares the figures of the captions with
the table. It does not see the screen and it does not hear the voice. A
person therefore reads the stage sheets and the video sheets against the
fact sheet and listens to every figure. The two checks find different faults. The script finds a changed digit
in a caption. The person finds a figure on a card that the narration does
not mention, or a spoken figure that differs from the caption.

1. Run the narration check on the final step script. It has to pass.
2. Give the fact sheet, the video sheets, and the video to a person who
   did not write the narration, with the owner of the source available
   for questions.
3. The person compares every figure on screen with the fact sheet, then
   listens to every figure in the narration and compares it with the
   caption.
4. Record each finding and its correction, and run the narration check
   again after any change.

**Worked example.** After the corrections of chapter 3, the narration
check found eleven figures in the table and no problem. The reviewer from
the labour statistics unit compared the four result cards with the
release table and found them correct, and heard "sixteen point eight" for
the corrected youth rate.

**What this does not do.** The review compares figures with the source.
Whether the video says the right things about the figures is the judgement
of the owner of the source, which the sign-off of recipe 6.4 records.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="6.2 Watch and listen to the whole video" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination officer and a second reviewer" time="Twenty minutes per video">

**Result:** two people have watched the whole video, once with sound on
headphones and once without sound with the captions shown, and the faults
found have been fixed.

The contact sheets show the state at the end of each beat. The viewing
shows what happens between: a transition that flashes, an animation that
is too fast to read, a figure that the voice reads before it appears, or
music that competes with the voice. WCAG 2.2 sets one limit that applies
to motion: nothing in the video flashes more than three times in any one
second (success criterion 2.3.1, level A). The viewing without sound tests
the captions as a deaf viewer meets them.

1. Watch the video with sound on headphones. Note any figure read before
   it appears, any line that is hard to hear over the music, and any word
   the voice mispronounces.
2. Watch it again without sound, with the captions on. Note any caption
   that disappears before it can be read and any caption that covers text
   on screen.
3. Check the length against the estimate of chapter 3 and the loudness
   with the measurement of chapter 5.
4. Fix the faults in the stage, the step script, or the captions, and
   record again where the stage or the narration changed.

**Worked example.** The viewing with sound found that the bar of the
employed persons card was still growing while its figure was read, which
the video sheets had shown and the team had fixed with a longer step. The
viewing without sound found that the captions covered the source line at
the bottom of the result scene, and the agent moved the source line up.
The video ran within a few seconds of the estimate.

**What this does not do.** Two viewers from the team can miss faults
that a viewer who relies on captions or on a screen reader would find.
An organization with a user panel asks one of its members to watch the
video before publication.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="6.3 Check the captions and publish them with a transcript" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination officer" time="An hour per video">

**Result:** the caption file passes the caption check, and the captions
and a transcript are ready to publish with the video.

Captions are the text of the audio, timed to it, shown on request by the
video player. The format used on the web is WebVTT, a W3C text format in
which each cue has a start time, an end time, and one or more lines of
text. The program's tool writes the caption file from the `say` fields,
one cue per narration step, with the whole step on one line. A player
shows such a cue as a block that covers much of the frame. The cues are
therefore divided into shorter cues before publication.

The limits of the check come from published subtitle guidelines. The BBC Subtitle Guidelines (version 1.2.5, March 2026) limit a line to
37 characters where subtitles are shown in Teletext. For online video they
express the limit as a share of the frame width, and they recommend at
most two lines for landscape video. The Netflix English (USA) Timed Text Style Guide
sets 42 characters per line, two lines, and a reading speed of up to 20
characters per second for adult programmes. The script uses 37 characters,
two lines, and 20 characters per second by default. It also checks the
timing rules of the WebVTT format: a cue ends after it starts, and no cue
starts before the start of the previous one. A cue that starts before the
previous one ends is allowed by the format, but it shows two captions at
once, and the script flags it.

1. Divide the tool's cues into short cues. The option `--rewrap` divides
   each cue at the ends of sentences and between words into cues of at
   most two lines of 37 characters, and shares the time of the original
   cue by the number of characters.
2. Run the caption check and correct each problem by hand: shorten or
   break a line, divide a cue, or move a start time.
3. Read the captions once against the narration, because a rewrapped line
   can break at an awkward point.
4. Write the transcript from the caption text. The catalogue record of
   chapter 7 does this from the same file, so the transcript and the
   captions say the same thing.

```bash
python check_captions.py captions.vtt
```

```text
captions.vtt: 59 cues, last cue ends at 00:03:30.881
reading speed: highest 17.5 characters per second; median 172 words per minute
problem: cue 46 (00:02:39.370): line of 68 characters (limit 37)
problem: cue 47 (00:02:43.000): starts before the previous cue ends at 00:02:43.256
result: FAIL
```

**Worked example.** The team's caption file had 59 cues after the rewrap,
and a hand edit had joined the two lines of the participation cue into one
of 68 characters. Another edit had moved the start of the employed persons
cue one second earlier, so that it overlapped the participation cue by a
quarter of a second. The team broke the line after "rose to" and moved the
start back, and the second run passed. On the program's own
Proof-Carrying Numbers explainer, the check finds that every one of the 34
cues written by the tool has a line longer than 37 characters, and one
cue reads at 20.2 characters per second. After the rewrap, the file has 79 cues and passes, with a highest reading speed of
19.9 characters per second.

**What this does not do.** The check measures lines, cues, and timing. It
does not check that a caption matches what the voice says, that a line
breaks at a natural point, or that the captions are placed clear of text
on screen. The viewing of recipe 6.2 checks those.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="6.4 Record the sign-off by a named person" level="Foundational" skills="Head of dissemination and the owner of the source" time="Fifteen minutes per video">

**Result:** one named person has decided that the video may be published,
and a record says who decided, on which version of the files, and on the
basis of which checks.

A sign-off is the organization's decision to publish, and it is made by
a person who can be asked about it later. It rests on the checks of this
chapter and on the owner of the source's confirmation that the video
states the facts correctly. Recording the version of the files that was
signed off matters because a video can be recorded again after the
sign-off, and the record shows whether the published file is the one that
was approved. A checksum, a short code computed from the content of a
file, identifies the version: a file that has changed has a different
checksum.

1. Collect the results: the narration check, the caption check, the
   findings of the viewing and their corrections, and the loudness.
2. Ask the owner of the source to confirm the fact sheet and the final
   video.
3. Compute the checksums of the video file and the caption file, for
   example with `sha256sum`.
4. Record the decision with the name and the role of the person, the
   date, the checksums, and the results. Keep the record with the video's
   files and link it from the catalogue record.

**Worked example.** The sign-off record of the release video names the
head of dissemination as the person who decided to publish on the day of
the release, with the confirmation of the head of the labour statistics
unit. The record holds the checksums of the video and caption files, the
passing runs of the two checks, and the two findings of the viewing with
their corrections. This guide shows roles in place of the names that the
organization's record carries.

**What this does not do.** The sign-off records a decision. It does not
make the video correct; the checks before it do, and the record makes
them traceable.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Caption files.** WebVTT for the web and for most video platforms; a broadcaster may ask for EBU-TT-D, the format the BBC guidelines specify for delivery.
- **Caption editors.** An open caption editor can divide and retime cues by hand, in place of or after the rewrap of recipe 6.3.
- **Transcript on the web page.** The transcript can sit below the video on the release page, or in an expandable section, so that it can be searched and quoted.
- **Sign-off in a workflow tool.** The record of recipe 6.4 can be an approval in the organization's content system, as long as it keeps the checksums and the results.

## World Bank examples

- The program's [production guide](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/blob/main/scripts/videos/README.md), section 8, gives the review checklist for its videos: contact sheets, motion, the greps of the writing rules on the narration, figures against the source, length and levels, and the MP4 and caption file sent together.
- The program's tool writes the WebVTT caption file of each video from the `say` fields of its step script, which is the file that recipe 6.3 divides into short cues.

## Common mistakes

- **Captions as one cue per narration step.** The player covers a large part of the frame with a block of text that changes every few seconds.
- **Only the script checks the figures.** A figure on a card or in the voice is outside what the script reads.
- **The video watched only with sound.** The captions are first seen by the viewers who depend on them.
- **A sign-off without the version.** The record cannot show that the published file is the one that was approved.

## Verification

- **Check runs.** The narration check and the caption check pass on the files of the published video.
- **Accessibility check.** The published video has captions that can be turned on in the player and a transcript on the web page.
- **Sign-off check.** The checksums in the sign-off record match the published video and caption files.
