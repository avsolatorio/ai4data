# 1. Topics suited to an explainer video

**Question:** When does an explainer video help, and when is a page, a chart, or a table enough?

## Rationale

A video costs more to make and to keep current than a page. It has to be
remade when the figures or the method change, and a reader cannot search
it, copy a figure from it, or read it at their own pace. A team that makes
a video of every release spends its time on videos that a table would
have served better, and it leaves outdated videos online.

Some topics gain from a video. A calculation that proceeds in steps, a
classification that sorts people or businesses into groups, or a method
that turns one kind of data into another is easier to follow when the
steps appear one after another with a voice that explains each. The
recurring questions that a user service receives about a release are
another sign: a question asked many times about what a figure means is a
question about a concept, and a concept can be explained once.

The decision is therefore made per topic, before any work starts, and it
is recorded. The record lets the team see later which videos exist, which
source each one depends on, and which ones need to be remade.

## Target state

- Each proposed video has passed a written test that says why a video serves the topic better than a page, a chart, or a table.
- Each video is assigned to one of two kinds: a concept explainer or a walkthrough of a website feature.
- The topics that stay as text, charts, or tables are recorded with the reason.
- The team keeps a topic list with the source, the owner, and the status of each video.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Test the first topic against the conditions of recipe 1.1 and choose its kind with recipe 1.2. |
| **AI-ready** | Keep the topic list of recipe 1.3, with the source and the owner of every video, and review it with the release calendar. |
| **AI-native** | Link each entry of the topic list to the identifiers of its source datasets, so that a new release or a revision marks the videos that depend on it. |

:::info[Running example]
The dissemination team of the example organization considers three
topics for its first video: the second-quarter 2025 Labour Force Survey
release, the youth unemployment rate on its own, and the release calendar
of the survey. The test of recipe 1.1 selects the release, because the
definitions of employment and unemployment and the calculation of the
rates proceed in steps. The youth rate stays as a chart on the release
page, and the calendar stays as a table.
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="1.1 Test a topic against the conditions for a video" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination officer with the owner of the source" time="An hour per topic">

**Result:** the team has a written decision for the topic: a video, or a
page, a chart, or a table, with the reason in one or two sentences.

An explainer video presents information in a fixed order and at a fixed
pace. That suits content in which each step depends on the one before,
and it suits readers who would not read a methodology note. It does not
suit content that a reader wants to look up, compare, or copy, because a
table or a chart shows all of it at once. The test below makes this
judgement explicit, so that two people on the team reach the same
decision for the same topic.

1. Write the topic as one sentence that names what the reader will
   understand after the video, for example "how the unemployment rate is
   calculated from the survey".
2. Check the four conditions. The topic has a sequence (steps, a
   classification, or a calculation). It has a published source with a
   named owner. Readers ask about it repeatedly, which the user service or
   the search log of the website can show. The method or the definitions
   change less often than the figures, so that the video stays valid
   across several releases or can be remade with new figures.
3. Choose a video when all four conditions hold. Choose a chart when the
   point is a change over time or a comparison, a table when readers need
   to look up values, and a page of text when the topic is a list of
   facts without an order.
4. Write the decision and the reason in the topic list of recipe 1.3.

**Worked example.** The team wrote the release topic as "what the
second-quarter 2025 Labour Force Survey release reports, and how its rates
are defined". The topic has a sequence: the survey classifies each person,
and the rates are calculated from the groups. Its source is the release
table and the labour market report `EX-REP-2025-02`, owned by the labour
statistics unit. The user service had received repeated questions about
the difference between the unemployment rate and the share of adults
without a job. The definitions follow the international standard and
change rarely. All four conditions held, and the team chose a video. The
youth rate on its own failed the first condition, because it is one
figure with a previous value, and it stays as a chart.

**What this does not do.** The test decides whether a topic suits a video.
It does not estimate the audience or the cost. A team with a budget for
only a few videos ranks the topics that pass the test by the number of
questions the user service receives about each.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="1.2 Choose the kind of video for the topic" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination officer" time="Fifteen minutes per topic">

**Result:** each video in the topic list is a concept explainer or a site
walkthrough, and the choice determines how it will be built.

The program's production guide distinguishes two kinds of video. A concept explainer explains a method or an idea step by step, with animated scenes of its own. A
site walkthrough shows how to use a feature of a website, recorded from
the live site with a pointer and captions. The two are built differently.
A concept explainer needs a stage page with scenes (chapter 4), and a
walkthrough needs a script of clicks and pages on the live site. In the
program's practice, a walkthrough of a notebook page was tried for a
method and rejected, because it showed the page without explaining the
method.

1. Choose a concept explainer when the reader needs to understand a
   definition, a calculation, or a method.
2. Choose a site walkthrough when the reader needs to find, filter, or
   download something on the organization's website.
3. Where a topic has both parts, make two short videos. A concept
   explainer that also shows the website becomes long and dates quickly,
   because the website changes more often than the method.

**Worked example.** The release video is a concept explainer: it explains
the classification of the population and the calculation of the rates.
The team added a second entry to the topic list, a site walkthrough of how
to download the release table from the organization's data portal, to be
made after the portal's next redesign.

**What this does not do.** The recipe covers two kinds of video. An
interview, a recorded presentation, or a short clip for social media
follows other practices, which this guide does not cover.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="1.3 Keep a topic list with sources and owners" level="AI-ready" skills="Head of dissemination" time="An hour to set up, then minutes per video">

**Result:** the team has one table that lists every proposed, made, and
rejected video with its kind, its source, its owner, and its status.

A topic list is the register of the organization's videos. It records the
decision of recipe 1.1 for each topic, including the topics that stay as
text or charts, and it records the source of each video by its
identifier. The identifier lets the team find, at each new release, the
videos whose figures the release changes. The list has one row per
topic, with the following columns.

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Topic | The sentence of recipe 1.1 step 1. |
| Decision | Video, chart, table, or page, with the reason. |
| Kind | Concept explainer or site walkthrough. |
| Sources | Identifiers of the datasets and documents the video uses. |
| Owner | The role that answers for the facts. |
| Status | Proposed, in production, published, to be remade, or withdrawn. |

1. Create the list in the format the team already uses for its release
   calendar (a spreadsheet or a table in the content system).
2. Add every topic tested with recipe 1.1, including the rejected ones,
   so that the same topic is not proposed again without a new reason.
3. At each release, read the list for videos whose sources include the
   released dataset and mark them "to be remade" where the release changes a figure stated in the video (chapter 7).

**Worked example.** The team's list has three rows after the first
review.

| Topic | Decision | Kind | Sources | Owner | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The second-quarter 2025 release and the definitions of its rates | Video: the definitions and the calculation proceed in steps | Concept explainer | `EX-LFS-2025-Q2`, `EX-REP-2025-02` | Labour statistics unit | In production |
| The youth unemployment rate | Chart: one figure with a previous value | | `EX-LFS-2025-Q2` | Labour statistics unit | Published as a chart |
| Downloading the release table from the data portal | Video | Site walkthrough | | Web team | Proposed |

**What this does not do.** The list records sources by identifier. It does
not detect a change in a source by itself; the release calendar and the
catalogue record of chapter 7 are where a change becomes visible.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **A page with an embedded video.** The video is one element of the release page or the methodology page. The page keeps the table and the text, and the video explains the concept beside them.
- **A series of short videos.** A method with several parts becomes several videos of three to five minutes each, so that each can be remade on its own.
- **A walkthrough recorded from the live site.** The program's tool records a walkthrough with Playwright from a script of pages and clicks; the guide's section on site walkthroughs describes it.

## Common mistakes

- **A video for every release.** A video that repeats the figures of a table adds a file to remake each quarter and explains nothing that the table does not show.
- **A concept explainer that tours the website.** The website changes more often than the method, and the video dates with the first redesign.
- **A topic without an owner.** Nobody can check the facts of the video or say when it is out of date.
- **Rejected topics left unrecorded.** The same topic returns at the next planning meeting without a new reason.

## Verification

- **Decision check.** For each video in the topic list, a second member of the team reads the topic sentence and the reason and reaches the same decision.
- **Source check.** Every row of the topic list has at least one source identifier and an owner.
- **Review check.** The topic list was read at the last release, and the status of each video reflects that release.
