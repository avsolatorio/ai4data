# Practical Guide to Explainer Videos Made with AI

**A cookbook for dissemination and communication teams in national statistical organizations.** Version 0.1 (working draft), October 2026.

:::note[Working draft]
This guide is a working draft from the AI for Data – Data for AI program. It
collects practical approaches and open standards. It does not
represent official World Bank Group guidance or policy and is not legal
advice; an organization applies it under its own statistical law, data
protection rules, and institutional policies.
Comments and corrections are welcome as
[GitHub issues](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/issues), and the
[contributing](./contributing.md) page explains how to propose a recipe.
:::

A statistical organization explains its methods, indicators, and releases
in methodology notes, release pages, and press briefings. A short
narrated video can reach readers who do not open those documents, and it
can show a calculation step by step in a way that a paragraph cannot.
A video made in a studio needs a script writer, an animator, a narrator,
and recording time, which a dissemination team does not always have. This guide
describes a way of producing explainer videos with a small team. An AI
agent drafts the narration and builds the animated scenes as a web page,
a synthetic voice reads the narration, and a browser records the page.
People in the organization collect the facts, check every figure, review
the result, and sign it off.

The guide is organized around seven questions that a dissemination team
asks: which topics suit a video, how a video is planned from its source,
how the narration is written, how the visuals are built, which voice reads
the narration, how the video is reviewed and made accessible, and how it
is published and kept current. Each chapter answers one question with
recipes, and the scripts of the guide check the parts of the work that a
script can check: the figures in the narration, the length, the caption
file, and the catalogue record.

The recipes build on published standards. The Web Content Accessibility
Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 set the requirements for captions, audio
description, and media alternatives. The W3C WebVTT format carries the
captions, and the schema.org VideoObject type describes the video in a
catalogue. Subtitle guidelines of a public broadcaster set the limits for
caption lines and reading speed. The [standards page](./standards.md)
lists each source and the chapters that use it. The production practice
follows the program's own explainer videos and the
[video tool](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/tree/main/scripts/videos)
with which they were made.

## Prerequisites

The recipes assume the following are in place or can be arranged:

- A published source for each video: a release table, a methodology note, or a documentation page, with a named owner who can answer questions about it.
- A dissemination team with one person who writes the narration and one who reviews it, and access to an AI agent that can write and run code on a workstation (a coding assistant with file and terminal access).
- A workstation where a browser, a text-to-speech model, and the video tool `ffmpeg` can be installed without administrator rights, as the program's tool requires.
- At the AI-ready level, a written production guide for the organization's videos and a catalogue in which each video has a record. At the AI-native level, the checks of this guide run as part of the production, and the catalogue record is generated from the same files as the video.

## First steps

For a team with limited time, this sequence gives the most useful result first:

1. Choose one topic with the test of [chapter 1](./topics.mdx), for example the latest labour force release. This takes an hour.
2. Write the fact sheet and the scene outline of [chapter 2](./planning.mdx) from the release table and the methodology note. This takes half a day.
3. Write the narration and run the check of [chapter 3](./narration.mdx). This takes a day for a first video.
4. Build, record, and review the video with [chapters 4](./visuals.mdx), [5](./voice.mdx), and [6](./review.mdx). This takes two to three days for a first video.

After the first video, the production guide of chapter 4 and the catalogue record of [chapter 7](./publishing.mdx) let the team make the next videos in the same form.

## Self-assessment

The [self-assessment](./start-here.mdx) takes about five minutes and places
a team's practice with explainer videos at a level in each chapter.

## Maturity levels

Each chapter describes three levels. An organization can sit at different
levels in different chapters, and the first level is a complete, useful
state on its own.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | The organization has made one explainer video from a fact sheet, with captions, a transcript, a disclosure of the synthetic voice, and a named sign-off. |
| **AI-ready** | Videos follow a written production guide, the narration and caption checks run on every video, and each video has a catalogue record linked to its sources. |
| **AI-native** | The step script, the stage page, the captions, and the catalogue record come from one set of files, the checks run on every change, and a change in the source data marks the videos that need to be remade. |

## Running example

The example organization of this series, the National Statistics Office
(example), makes an explainer video of its Labour Force Survey release for
the second quarter of 2025 (survey round `EX-LFS-2025-Q2`, report
`EX-REP-2025-02`). The release table gives four headline figures: the
unemployment rate of 6.1 percent after 6.3 percent, which is provisional;
youth unemployment of 16.8 percent after 17.5 percent; labour force
participation of 61.2 percent after 60.9 percent; and 4,312 thousand
employed persons after 4,268 thousand. The guide follows the team from the
choice of the topic to the published video and its catalogue record. The
figures are those of the shared example of this series. The narration,
the captions, and the addresses under `stats.example` are invented for
the example.

## Scope of the examples

The examples are small on purpose: one release table, one narration
script of 28 steps, one caption file, and one video card, so that a reader
can run the checks in an hour and see what each one finds. They are
starting points. Each recipe ends with a **What this does not do** note
that says where the example stops and what the full version adds. The
scripts compare figures, count words and characters, and fill a record.
The decisions on the topic, the wording, the visuals, and the sign-off are
made by people.
