# 3. Narration script

**Question:** How is the narration written for the voice and the captions?

## Rationale

The narration of an explainer is heard once, at the speed of the voice. A
listener cannot go back to the start of a long sentence, and a figure read
in an unexpected form ("four thousand three hundred twelve thousand") is
lost. The narration is therefore written for listening: one idea per
sentence, short sentences, and figures written out in the words the voice
will say. The same narration is shown as captions, so it is also written
for reading, with figures in digits as the release table prints them.

The program's tool handles the two forms with two fields per step. The
field `say` holds the text of the caption, with figures in digits. The
field `speak` holds the spoken form where it differs, with figures in
words. Both are written from the fact sheet of chapter 2, so that the
caption and the voice state the same facts. The captions are generated
from `say`, which keeps the caption file in step with the narration.

A narration drafted by an AI agent is fluent and sometimes wrong. It can
carry a figure that is close to the one in the table, write a figure in
digits in the spoken form, or join three ideas in one sentence. These are
problems that a script can find, before the recording and before a person
spends time listening.

## Target state

- The narration is a step script in which each step is one beat on screen with one or two sentences.
- Every figure in the captions is in the release table, and every figure in the spoken form is written in words.
- No sentence is longer than the word limit the organization has set.
- The estimated length is between three and five minutes.
- The narration check of recipe 3.3 passes before the video is recorded.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Write the narration under the rules of recipe 3.1 with the spoken forms of recipe 3.2, and run the check of recipe 3.3 once before recording. |
| **AI-ready** | Let the agent draft the narration from the fact sheet and the outline, and run the check on every draft. |
| **AI-native** | Run the check on every change to the step script, together with the caption check of chapter 6, so that a draft that fails cannot be recorded. |

## Recipes

<Recipe title="3.1 Write the narration as a step script with one idea per sentence" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination officer or writer" time="A day for a first video">

**Result:** the narration exists as a step script in which each step is
one change on screen and one or two sentences that describe it.

A step script is a JSON file with one entry per beat. The program's tool
reads it in order: it runs the instruction of the step in the stage page
(for example `go(8,5)`, which shows beat 5 of scene 8), waits, reads the
narration aloud, and moves on. Writing the narration step by step ties
each sentence to what is on screen at that moment, which is the rule that
the narration and the animation describe the same thing.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| `do` | The kind of step: `goto` opens the stage page, `eval` runs an instruction in it. |
| `js` | The instruction, `go(scene, beat)`. |
| `say` | The caption text, with figures in digits. |
| `speak` | The spoken form, where it differs from `say`. |
| `settle`, `pause`, `min` | Seconds before the narration, after it, and the least time on the beat. |

The rules below come from the program's production guide and the
writing rules of the project.

1. Write one idea per sentence, with a named subject and a verb, and at
   most two sentences per step.
2. Describe what is on screen as it appears. A sentence about a chart
   that the viewer cannot yet see is lost.
3. State only facts from the fact sheet. Say the status of a figure where
   the source gives one.
4. Avoid rhetorical questions, announcements of structure ("there are
   three steps"), and closing slogans. A viewer hears them as filler.
5. Read the whole narration aloud once before building anything.

**Worked example.** One step of the running example reads as follows.

```json
{
  "do": "eval",
  "js": "go(8,5)",
  "say": "The number of employed persons was 4,312 thousand, after 4,268 thousand in the first quarter.",
  "speak": "The number of employed persons was four million, three hundred and twelve thousand, after four million, two hundred and sixty-eight thousand in the first quarter.",
  "pause": 0.35
}
```

The step shows the fifth beat of the result scene, the employed persons
card, and the sentence names the figure on the card, its unit, and its
previous value.

**What this does not do.** The rules make the narration easy to follow.
They do not make it correct; the figures are checked by recipe 3.3 and by
the review of chapter 6.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="3.2 Write every figure in its spoken form" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination officer or writer" time="An hour per video">

**Result:** every step whose caption contains a figure, a year, or an
abbreviation read as letters has a `speak` field with that text written
out in words.

A text-to-speech model decides by itself how to read "6.1", "2025", or
"4,312 thousand", and its choice can differ between versions of the model
or between languages. Writing the spoken form removes the choice. The
spoken form also lets the team say a figure in the way the organization
says it in a briefing, for example "four million, three hundred and
twelve thousand" for 4,312 thousand persons.

1. Write decimals digit by digit after the point: "six point one percent".
2. Write years as they are said: "twenty twenty-five".
3. Write large figures with their unit in the form used in speech: 4,312
   thousand becomes "four million, three hundred and twelve thousand".
4. Space out abbreviations that are read as letters ("P C N") and write
   addresses as they are said ("stats dot example").
5. Keep the words of `speak` in the same order as those of `say`, so
   that the caption and the voice can be compared line by line.

**Worked example.** The step on participation in the agent's draft reads
`"speak": "Labour force participation rose to 61.2 percent, after sixty
point nine percent."`. The first figure is still in digits, and the voice
would read it as the model chooses. The team corrected it to "sixty-one
point two percent".

**What this does not do.** The recipe covers English. A narration in
another language follows that language's spoken forms for figures and
dates, and a speaker of the language reviews them.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="3.3 Check the figures, the spoken forms, and the length by script" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination officer" time="A minute per run">

**Result:** the narration has passed the narration check. Every figure in
the captions is in the release table, no figure is left in digits for the
voice, no sentence exceeds the word limit, and the estimated length is
between three and five minutes.

The script `check_narration.py` reads the step script and the release
table. It extracts each figure from the `say` fields, leaving out
reference periods, years, and ages, and looks it up among the values and
the previous values of the table. It flags digits in a `speak` field, and
a `say` with digits that has no `speak`. It splits each `say` into
sentences and flags those over the limit, 25 words by default, which is
the organization's choice. It estimates the length from the spoken words.
The default rate is 3.1 words per second plus one second per step for the
settle and the pause. These are the values of the program's voice: on the
program's two explainers the estimate gives 4 min 40 s and 4 min 43 s,
and the recorded videos last 4 min 40 s and 4 min 47 s.

1. Run the script on the step script and the release table.
2. For each figure not in the table, find the row of the fact sheet and
   correct the figure, or remove the sentence.
3. Write out the digits that the script finds in `speak`, and split the
   long sentences.
4. Run the script again until it passes, then record.

```bash
python check_narration.py narration.json release_table.csv
```

```text
narration.json: 28 narrated steps, 546 spoken words
estimated length: 3 min 24 s at 3.1 words per second and 1.0 s per step (target 3 min 00 s to 5 min 00 s)
figures in say: 11; 10 in the table, 1 not
problem: step 23, go(8,3): 16.3 is not a value or a previous value in release_table.csv
problem: step 24, go(8,4): speak keeps the digits 61.2
problem: step 27, go(9,2): sentence of 32 words (limit 25)
result: FAIL
```

**Worked example.** The draft gave youth unemployment as 16.3 percent,
and the table gives 16.8; the agent had changed one digit. The team corrected the caption and the spoken form ("sixteen point
eight") and wrote out 61.2 in step 24. It split the sentence of step 27
into two: one on the comparisons that need the same definitions and
population, and one on the methodology note that describes them. The second run passed, with eleven
figures found in the table.

**What this does not do.** The script checks that each figure is in the
table. It does not check that the figure is attached to the right series,
that the spoken words state the same figure as the caption, or that a
provisional figure is called provisional. The review of chapter 6 checks
those, by reading against the fact sheet and by listening.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **The program's step script format.** JSON with `say` and `speak`, as in the [program's tool](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/tree/main/scripts/videos); the captions are generated from `say`.
- **Speech Synthesis Markup Language (SSML).** A W3C markup for text-to-speech in which numbers, dates, and pauses can be tagged; some voices support it. The `speak` field does the same work in plain text for a voice that does not.
- **A narration in a document.** A team without the tool writes the narration in a two-column table (caption, spoken form) and records it line by line, with the same checks run on an export of the table.

## World Bank examples

- The program's [production guide](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/blob/main/scripts/videos/README.md), section 6, sets the narration rules and the spoken forms, and records the measured rate of 3.1 words per second.

## Common mistakes

- **A figure close to the right one.** A changed digit reads naturally and is found only by comparison with the table.
- **Digits left for the voice.** The voice reads "61.2" in a form the team did not choose, and a later version of the model may read it differently.
- **Three ideas in one sentence.** The listener loses the first by the time the third arrives.
- **A narration that describes the next scene.** The viewer hears about a chart before it appears and cannot connect the two.

## Verification

- **Script check.** The narration check passes on the final step script.
- **Reading check.** A person who did not write the narration reads it aloud at the speed of the voice and stops at no sentence.
- **Length check.** The estimated length is between three and five minutes, and the recorded video in chapter 6 is within twenty seconds of the estimate.
